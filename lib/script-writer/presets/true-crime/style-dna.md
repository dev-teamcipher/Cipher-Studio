# True Crime — Style DNA
Learned: 2026-08-06 | corpus=8 transcripts (7 usable; 9I6a6OJonkA pulled a corrupted/non-English caption track — excluded) | teachers: STAYAWAKE, Unseen, The Missing Enigma

These three teachers share a spine (concrete cold-open, present-tense reconstruction, evidence
released in timed drips, one big turn) but split into three **selectable MODES**. Pick one per
script and commit to it — do not blend the narrator voices.

- **MODE A — Interrogation/Bodycam Analysis** (STAYAWAKE): JCS-style. The star is the *evidence* —
  911 audio, bodycam, interrogation room. Analytical, names the psychology ("red flag," "eco-up
  technique"). Payoff = confession + verdict.
- **MODE B — Emotional Documentary** (Unseen): The star is the *victim and the family*. Cross-cut
  timeline, aching human detail, rhetorical questions, ends on legacy/meaning + a teaser for the
  next case. Best for abductions/survivors.
- **MODE C — Unsolved Wilderness Mystery** (The Missing Enigma): Conversational first-person
  narrator talking straight to camera. The star is the *question*. Lays out theories, resolves
  nothing, hands the verdict to the viewer. Best for disappearances with no answer.

---

## 1. Hook / cold-open
First 15–30s is a concrete scene with a named person, a real date/place, and a planted doom-clock —
never a throat-clearing intro or a "hey guys, today we're covering." Consensus templates (our words):

- **Ordinary-moment-plus-timer (A):** "On the morning of [date], [Name] walked the aisles of [store]
  buying [mundane thing]. She had no idea that in 40 minutes she would be dead." Mundane detail +
  countdown to death.
- **Cold-audio drop (A):** Open on a raw 911/bodycam line ("911, what's your emergency —") before
  any narration, then step back and explain who's calling.
- **Last-words quote (B):** Open on the victim's or a survivor's line in quotes ("The last thing I
  told her was: stay with your brother.") then reveal it's the last thing ever said.
- **Doom-foreshadow on happy footage (B):** Describe a birthday/home video, then: "The most
  disturbing part is this was filmed just weeks before her abduction."
- **Direct-address curiosity (C):** "Got an interesting one for you. I first heard about this when
  someone said it might be a death by Bigfoot — I'll let you decide." Frame the mystery, promise no
  easy answer.

Always seed ONE unanswered question in the first minute (Where is she? Why did the footage cut out?
Who is the man with her car?) and do not answer it until deep in.

## 2. Story structure (beat map)
Roughly consistent across teachers (percent = share of runtime):

1. **Cold-open scene + doom-clock** (0–8%): concrete moment, name, date, the question.
2. **Rewind / who they were** (8–20%): victim humanized in a few specific strokes (loved her kids,
   retired but still helped elders, a hiker of 32 years). Establish stakes so the loss lands.
3. **Chronological discovery** (20–55%): investigators/family walk it forward in dated, timestamped
   beats ("at 8:41 a.m. … three minutes later …"). Evidence dripped one clue at a time.
4. **The turn** (~55–70%): the piece that breaks the innocent story — the gun in the wrong room, the
   3-minute gap in the footage, the loyalty-card that names the stranger. Everything reframes.
5. **Pressure / interrogation / theories** (70–88%): confront the suspect (A), widen to the pattern
   and other victims (A/B), or lay competing theories side by side (C).
6. **Payoff** (88–97%): verdict + sentence (A), rescue/legacy/law-change (B), or the honest
   "still unsolved, you decide" (C).
7. **Reflective close + next hook** (last 3%): a line of meaning, then (B especially) a hard cut into
   the opening scene of the NEXT case to chain the binge.

## 3. Voice & sentence rhythm
- **Tense:** present-tense reconstruction is the default engine ("Amber grabs her bike and heads
  out"), even for decades-old cases. MODE A slips to past for the verdict/aftermath.
- **Length:** mostly medium declaratives, broken by short punches. Deploy one 4–8 word gut-punch per
  beat: "But there were no tears." / "She was gone." / "Then all of a sudden the footage cuts out."
- **Direct address:** MODE C uses "you"/"I" freely; A & B use "you" sparingly for the audience
  ("You see it pop up on your phone").
- **Rhetorical questions** carry transitions and cliffhangers: "If she was holding the gun, how did
  it end up in another room?" End sections on the question, not the answer.
- **Numbers are dramatized:** exact times, distances, counts ("4 miles from where she was
  abducted," "1,118 days," "22 firearms") — precision reads as authority and raises tension.

## 4. Retention devices
- **Open loops:** plant a concrete unknown early (missing 3 minutes; the man with the birthmark) and
  keep referencing it before you resolve it.
- **Foreshadow-then-payoff:** "as you'll find out later, Daniel had a pattern" — promise a future
  reveal to pull the viewer forward.
- **Escalation stacking:** "the allegations against him were stacking higher and higher" — list
  corroborating witnesses/evidence so the case visibly tightens.
- **Pattern interrupt via raw audio:** cut from smooth narration into a jagged 911/bodycam/
  interrogation quote to reset attention every few minutes.
- **Red-flag callouts (A):** name the tell in real time ("another red flag for the officer") so the
  viewer feels like the detective.
- **Mini-recaps** before the turn: restate the suspect's version right before you demolish it.

## 5. Transitions
Connective phrases that move sections without dead air:
- Time jumps: "Three minutes later…", "One week after issuing the bolo…", "23 years later…"
- Reveal turns: "But what's more shocking is…", "That's when everything changed.",
  "Then all of a sudden…"
- Escalation: "To make matters worse…", "And most damning of all…"
- Contrast/doubt: "Chris's story immediately set off red flags.", "But the details contradicted the
  footage."
- Widening scope: "But Edna may not have been his only victim."

## 6. Vocabulary & tone
- Recurring diction: "red flag," "foul play," "person of interest," "broad daylight," "in cold
  blood," "monster," "House of Horrors," "tirelessly," "desperately," "chilling," "gruesome
  discovery," "shocking twist."
- Register: A = analytical-detached but damning; B = warm, grieving, reverent toward the victim;
  C = casual, curious, humble about uncertainty.
- **Avoid:** speculation stated as fact, gory gratuitousness for its own sake, "hey guys" YouTuber
  filler, jokes, and — in MODE C — pretending to solve what is genuinely open. Name what is proven
  vs. theorized ("police believed," "authorities theorized").

## 7. Outro / CTA
- No cheesy "smash that like button." Close on **meaning**: the verdict and what it cost the family
  (A), the legacy/law that outlived the victim — Amber Alert (B), or an open invitation to weigh in
  (C: "let me know what you think happened").
- Where a case is unsolved/active, end with the real tip line and case number — it doubles as a
  moral CTA and a credibility signal.
- MODE B chains: the last 10 seconds start the NEXT case's cold-open to auto-roll the binge.

## 8. Title & thumbnail angles
Recurring framings seen in the outlier pool:
- **Killer-doesn't-know-yet:** "Killer Husband Realizes Wife Is Still Breathing," "Killer Thinks He
  Got Away — Doesn't Realize CCTV Caught Him." Dramatic irony in the title.
- **Child-outsmarts-predator:** "9 YO Uses True Crime Skills to Manipulate Captor," "8 YO Traps Him
  at Denny's," "Twins Outsmart Killer Mom." (Unseen's strongest lever.)
- **The clock:** "It Only Took 8 Minutes."
- **The overlooked clue:** "Until 6YO Reveals This Clue," "Bizarre Facebook Post Led Detectives to…"
- **The gruesome discovery / secret life:** "Southern Couple Discovers House of Horrors," "Gruesome
  Discovery Reveals Secret Life."
- Thumbs pair a real face/still with a caught-red-handed or found-the-body implication; caption
  states the irony the title sets up.

## WRITER CHECKLIST
1. Pick ONE mode (A interrogation / B emotional / C unsolved) and hold its voice for the whole script.
2. Cold-open inside a concrete named scene with a real date and place — no intro, no "hey guys."
3. Plant a doom-clock or unanswered question in the first 60 seconds; don't answer it until past the midpoint.
4. Write the reconstruction in present tense with exact timestamps ("at 8:41 a.m. … three minutes later…").
5. Humanize the victim in 2–3 specific concrete strokes before the crime, so the loss has weight.
6. Drip evidence one clue at a time — never front-load the solution; each beat earns the next.
7. Land ONE clear "turn" past the midpoint where the innocent version collapses; reframe what came before.
8. Drop one 4–8 word gut-punch line per beat and end sections on a rhetorical question, not its answer.
9. Break narration every few minutes with a raw 911 / bodycam / interrogation quote as a pattern interrupt.
10. Separate proven from theorized out loud ("police believed," "authorities theorized") — never state speculation as fact.
11. Close on meaning (verdict + cost / legacy / open question), plus the real tip line if the case is unsolved.
12. In MODE B, end by hard-cutting into the next case's cold-open to chain the binge.
